Church roofing in Knoxville and East Tennessee

A church often has two or three roofs on one building: a steep sanctuary, a flat fellowship hall or education wing, and the walls and valleys where they meet. Each needs a different material, and the work has to fit around Sunday.

Steep and flat on one building

Sanctuary roofs are often tall and steep, which means more safety gear and more time than a house, and a higher price per square. Most are architectural shingles or metal. Fellowship halls and classroom wings are usually low-slope, and those take TPO, EPDM or a coating; commercial roofing explains the four systems.

Church leaks tend to start where the roofs meet, like the wall where a flat roof butts into the sanctuary or a valley between additions built decades apart. The base of a steeple or bell tower is the other usual suspect.

Scheduling around services

Roof work stops for Sunday. Tear-off is loud, so it runs on weekdays, and the schedule works around funerals and weddings once the church office shares the calendar. The job plan covers which doors stay open and where the dumpster sits so it doesn't take the parking lot. The building is dried in before the crew leaves each day. A weekday preschool or school in the building changes the plan, so mention it early.

For the building committee

Committees decide together, and they need a price they can read at a meeting. The written price breaks the job out by roof section, so the sanctuary and the hall each carry their own number. That makes it possible to fix the worst section this year and budget the rest, or to phase the work to match a capital campaign.

Storm damage on a church roof goes through the church's property insurer, and the steps are close to a homeowner claim: see how a claim works. To spread the cost out, ask about financing.

Questions about church roofs

Our steeple leaks. Is that roofing work?

Partly. A steeple can leak at its louvers, at the joints in its cladding, or at the base where the tower meets the roof. The base flashing is roofing work, and we can repair it. Work higher up the steeple, or a steeple that has to come down for repair, needs a specialty crew and often a crane. If that's what it takes, we'll say so before anything is priced.

Can you look at the other buildings on the property too?

Yes. A parsonage or a storage building on the same property can be looked at during the same inspection, with each building on its own line of the written price. The committee can then decide one building at a time, and a parsonage roof is usually a plain residential job.

Who should be our point of contact?

One person the committee names: someone who can unlock the building and answer questions during the week. Prices and schedule changes go to that person first, with copies to whoever else the committee wants. A single contact keeps a roof job from stalling between meetings.

Can you work around our church's services and events?

Yes. Tell us what's on the calendar (Sunday services, weddings, funerals, Vacation Bible School) and the loud work gets planned around it. Many churches have a steep sanctuary roof and a flat fellowship hall roof, and we price both. A building committee gets a written scope with each part of the job and its cost.

Why do roofing quotes vary so much?

Because they often aren't pricing the same roof. One quote includes full tear-off, ice-and-water membrane in the valleys, and fresh pipe boots and flashing. Another reuses the flashing, skips the membrane and bills rotted decking later. Ask each roofer for a line-by-line price and compare line to line.

Can I pay for my roof over time?

Ask us. We don't offer a lender program today, so tell us when you book that you'd like to spread out the cost. You'll get a written price for your bank or credit union. Common choices are a home improvement loan, a credit union personal loan, or a home equity line of credit.
